### Who bears the dilution

A point that usually causes misunderstanding and is better cleared up now:

| | Before | After | Dilution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founders | 100% | 85% | **−15 points** |
| — of which, from the pool | | | −10 points |
| — of which, from the investor | | | −5 points |

**The option pool is created pre-money.** That means the 10% dilution is borne
entirely by the founders, not by the investor — who comes in after the pool
already exists. It is standard practice, but it is negotiable: a pool created
post-money dilutes everyone proportionally.

**It is worth negotiating.** The difference, in this round, is about 5 percentage
points for the founders.

## 5. The split between founders

The table above assumes **50/50**. It is the most common split and the one that
causes the most problems.

**The problem:** in a disagreement there is no tie-breaking mechanism. The
company locks up exactly when it needs to decide quickly. It is one of the most
frequent causes of early-stage startup failure, and it has nothing to do with the
merit of either person.

**Recommended alternative:** a slightly asymmetric split, agreed now, while
agreeing it still costs nothing.

| Option | João | Leonardo | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — 50/50 | 42.5% | 42.5% | Simple, no tie-break |
| **B — 52/48** | 44.2% | 40.8% | **Recommended** — minimal tie-break |
| C — 55/45 | 46.75% | 38.25% | If the contributions are clearly different |

Criteria for deciding: who had the initial idea, who has put in more time so far,
who takes the CEO role, who goes full-time first.

**If you choose 50/50**, include a tie-breaking clause in the shareholders'
agreement — for example, an independent third party who decides in a deadlock, or
a casting vote for the CEO on operational matters.

## 6. ⚠️ Structural question: Lda or SA?

**This decision has to be taken before incorporation.**

A **private limited company (Lda)** has no shares — it has **quotas**. Assigning
a quota generally requires a notarised or certified private document and
registration, and is subject to the other shareholders' pre-emption rights. That
makes an employee option plan (ESOP) **operationally heavy**: every option
exercise is a quota assignment, with costs and formalities.

A **public limited company (SA)** has shares, transferable by mere registration,
allows different classes of shares and naturally accommodates option plans and
successive investor entries.

| | Lda | SA |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum capital | €1 | €50,000 |
| Cost and speed of incorporation | Lower | Higher |
| Transfer of holdings | Heavy | Simple |
| Practical viability of an ESOP | **Difficult** | **Natural** |
| VC investor preference | Lower | **Higher** |

**Recommendation:** incorporate as an **Lda** if the ESOP can wait and the round
is small — it is cheaper and faster. Convert to an **SA** before the first
institutional round. Conversion is possible and routine, but it has a cost and
takes time: **deciding in advance avoids doing it under the time pressure of a
round**, which is when it gets expensive.

Confirm with a corporate lawyer and an accountant before proceeding.

## 7. Founder vesting

**Not negotiable, and it should be in the shareholders' agreement from
incorporation.**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Period | 4 years |
| Cliff | 12 months |
| Vesting after the cliff | Monthly (1/48 per month) |
| Acceleration on acquisition | 50% (single trigger) |
| Bad leaver clause | Forfeiture of unvested holdings |

**Why, even between people who trust each other:** if one of the two leaves after
8 months, with no vesting they take half the company and the other works for
years for an absent partner. No institutional investor enters a company without
founder vesting — they will require it at the round, and by then the negotiation
is far harder than it is today, while the company is still worth nothing.

## 8. A note on the size of the round

US$50,000 for 5% is a small round — in practice, somewhere between friends and
family and an individual angel.

**Implications to keep in mind:**

- At a US$1M valuation, US$50,000 is a short runway. At the cost rate in the
  financial plan it covers a few months of operation.
- A low valuation in a first round **constrains the next ones**. If the following
  round is at US$4M, that is a healthy multiple; if the company needs to raise
  before making progress, the risk is a down round.
- **An alternative worth considering:** a convertible instrument (a SAFE or a
  convertible note) with a valuation cap, which defers setting the price until
  there are metrics. For a pre-revenue company it is usually more favourable to
  the founders than fixing US$1M now.

It is worth discussing this alternative with the investor before fixing a price.
